Circa

How this works

Circa takes a place and a date and shows you the history that was within reach of that place at that time. Here is what that actually means.

What you give it

A kind of life event, a place, and a date. One birth or death is required, because a life needs an anchor in time before anything can be measured against it. Everything else is optional: somewhere they lived, a marriage, a death.

Partial answers are expected rather than tolerated. A year on its own is enough. So is a country on its own. Circa will tell you when it has matched a country or a state rather than a town, because that changes how much you should trust a distance, and you should be told rather than left to guess.

What it does with it

Your events become spans: stretches of time attached to a location. A birth in one town and a death in another sixty years later describe a life that has to be somewhere in between, and each span is looked at separately.

For each span, Circa asks the history record a narrow question: what was close enough, and recent enough, to have been part of that life.

What “within reach” means

This is the part that is easy to get backwards. Circa does not draw a circle around your town and collect whatever falls inside it. The circle belongs to the event, not to you.

Every historic event in the record carries its own reach — how far its influence plausibly extended. A county fair reaches the county. A world war reaches everywhere. An event appears in your timeline when your place falls inside its reach.

That single decision explains most of what the timeline does. It is why something enormous and far away can outrank something small and close: the distant event's reach covers your town comfortably, and it mattered more. It is also why a genuinely local event, which would lose on significance to anything national, still earns its place — it is one of the few things whose reach was small enough to be about your town in particular.

Why two towns an hour apart differ

Because reaches are of wildly different sizes, an hour's drive crosses the edge of the small ones and none of the large ones. Two towns that close will share their national and global history almost exactly, and will diverge sharply on everything local. If two neighbouring towns returned identical timelines, Circa would be doing nothing more than looking up a decade.

Why a famous birth does not crowd out a local flood

Notable births and deaths are drawn from their own allowance rather than competing with local history for the same slots. Left to compete, well-known people win on significance nearly every time, and the result is a timeline of celebrities who happened to be born nearby instead of a portrait of a place.

The handful of events that reached everyone

A few moments were not really about a place at all. The moon landing, the end of a world war, the eruption that changed the weather for a year: if you were alive, it was part of your life, whether you lived in a capital or on a farm a hundred miles from the nearest train.

Circa keeps a small, hand-picked set of these and shows them wherever they fall inside a life. They are marked World, and they do not take a slot away from anything local: they are drawn in addition to the events that were chosen by reach, not in competition with them. So a timeline centred on a quiet county still carries the moments that everyone alive carried, and still keeps room for the flood nobody outside the county remembers.

Narrowing what you see

Once a timeline has been built, the chips above it let you read it from different angles. Reach narrows by how widely an event mattered, from Local up to World. Kind narrows by what sort of event it was, such as People, Conflict or Discoveries. The two can be mixed.

This happens in your browser, on the timeline you already have, so it is instant and nothing is thrown away. Switch a chip back on and those events return exactly as they were. It is worth trying Local on its own at least once: it is the version of a timeline that only your place could have produced.

Why some cards carry only a year

Circa keeps whatever precision the record actually holds instead of filling in the rest. A card reading 1725 means the year is known and the day is not. It is a smaller claim, made honestly, and it is a better answer than a confident date nobody can support.

Why a long event appears twice

A war that ran for seven years touched a life twice, so Circa shows it where it began and again where it ended, each card labelled accordingly. Two cards naming the same war are usually its two ends rather than a mistake. Genuine duplicates do still turn up, and the FAQ says what to do about them.

A worked example

Take a birth in a small town in the 1910s and a death in a city seventy years later. That is two spans. The first pulls in the things that reached a small town in that decade — the arrival of a railway, a flood, a mine opening — alongside the national events large enough to reach anywhere. The second pulls in a city's history, which is denser, and where the local tier has far more competition.

Read end to end, the two together are a rough answer to a question that is otherwise hard to ask: what was going on around this person while they were alive.

What it is not

Circa is not genealogy. It does not know anything about the person, holds no records about them, and cannot tell you whether they were present at any of this. It describes the surroundings of a life, not the life.

The history record is broad rather than complete, and it is thinner the further back and the further from large cities you go. An empty local tier usually means the record is quiet there, not that nothing happened.
