Frequently Asked Questions

Does Circa keep what I type?

No accounts, no sign-in, no saved searches. There is nowhere in Circa to enter a person's name — the form asks for a place and a date, and that is all it has ever asked for. So there is no history of who you looked up, because Circa was never told.

Nothing personal is kept, and nothing is kept on a server. Your browser may remember small conveniences for you, like which filter chips you last had switched on, and a timeline you choose to share carries its details inside the link so the page can rebuild itself. There is no account, no profile and no record here for any of it to attach to.

Can I see only local events, or only the big ones?

Yes. Above every timeline there are two rows of chips. Reach narrows by how widely an event mattered, from Local up to World, and Kind narrows by what sort of event it was, such as People, Conflict or Discoveries. You can mix the two.

Filtering happens in your browser on the timeline you already have, so it is instant and nothing is lost. Switch a chip back on and those events come straight back. Turning everything except Local off is a lovely way to read just the texture of a place.

Why am I seeing the same event twice?

Usually because a long event is showing you both of its ends. A war that ran for seven years touched a life twice, so it appears where it began and again where it finished. That pair is working as intended.

Sometimes it is a genuine double, where the same moment reached Circa through two different sources and arrived as two cards. A large clean-up in September 2026 merged a few hundred of these, and more will follow as they are found. If you spot one, the suggestion link at the foot of your timeline is the quickest way to tell us.

Why did my first timeline take so long?

The history service rests when nobody is using it, and waking it up can take the better part of a minute. The first timeline of the day is the slow one. Everything after it is quick.

Circa starts waking the service while you fill in the form, so the wait is usually hidden.

Why is the moon landing on my small-town timeline?

Because a handful of moments reached everyone alive at the time, and Circa shows those wherever your person was. They are marked World in the Reach chips, and they are there on purpose: they were part of that life too, however far from the centre of things it was lived.

If you would rather read the timeline without them, switch the World chip off.

Can I send someone my timeline, and will the link keep working?

Yes to both, and for the same reason. The life events travel inside the link itself rather than pointing at something saved on a server. Whoever opens it gets the same events filled in, and the timeline rebuilds itself in front of them.

Because there is nothing stored for the link to point at, there is nothing that can expire or be cleaned up later. A link you send today still works in a year.

The one thing worth knowing: the details are in the link, so treat it the way you would treat the timeline itself. Anyone you send it to can see it, which is rather the point.

What happens when I rate a tile?

It goes straight into making the history better. How far an event reaches and how much it mattered are scored in GeoHistory, the dataset underneath this app, and those scores are the whole reason your timeline looks the way it does.

A thumbs down on a tile that clearly has nothing to do with the life in front of you is the most useful thing you can tell us: it usually means that event's reach is set too wide, or it is scored as more significant than it really was. Enough of those and the score gets corrected for everyone.

Thumbs up is just as valuable and gets clicked far less. It confirms that a surprising result was a good one — exactly the kind of thing that is easy to tune away by accident while chasing the bad ones.

Circa records which result you rated and which way. That is the entire vote.

What if I only know a year? Or the person is still living?

A year on its own is fine, and so is a country on its own. Circa is built for the shape of the information people actually have, which is usually partial.

For someone still living, give the birth and add somewhere they have lived. A death is only one of two ways to anchor a life; it is not required.

The less precise the place, the more carefully you should read the distances. A country matches from its centre, which can be a long way from anywhere a person actually stood, and Circa says so when it happens.

The same goes for the history itself. A card showing only a year means the year is all the record gives, and Circa would rather show you a smaller, honest claim than invent a day to make the page look tidy.

My town returned nothing local. Is it broken?

Probably not. The history record is broad rather than complete, and it thins out the further back you go and the further you get from large cities. A quiet local tier usually means the record is quiet there, not that the town had no history.

The national and global events in your timeline are unaffected — those reach everywhere, including places the record has little to say about.

Getting more local history into the record is the work we care most about, because it is the part of a timeline nobody else can tell you. If you know something that belongs there, please suggest it from the foot of your timeline.

Where does the history come from?

Wikidata and Wikipedia, assembled into a dataset by the GeoHistory project. The Resources page has the details and the links.

How does it decide what to show?

Every event carries its own reach, and it appears when your place falls inside it — which is why a distant war can outrank a nearby fair, and why two neighbouring towns get different answers. How this works explains it properly.
